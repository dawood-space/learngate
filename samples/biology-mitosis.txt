Mitosis is the process of cell division where a single eukaryotic cell divides into two genetically identical daughter cells.
The cell cycle consists of interphase and the M phase, which includes mitosis and cytokinesis.
During interphase, the cell grows, replicates its DNA in the S phase, and prepares for division in the G2 phase.
Mitosis is divided into four main stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase.
In prophase, chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes and the nuclear envelope begins to break down.
The centrosomes move to opposite poles of the cell and assemble the mitotic spindle fibers.
In metaphase, chromosomes line up along the equatorial plane of the cell known as the metaphase plate.
Spindle fibers attach to the kinetochores located at the centromere of each chromosome.
During anaphase, sister chromatids are pulled apart toward opposite poles by the shortening spindle microtubules.
In telophase, nuclear membranes reform around each set of separated chromosomes at the cell poles.
The chromosomes begin to decondense back into diffuse chromatin during telophase.
Cytokinesis completes the cell division process by dividing the cytoplasm into two separate individual cells.
In animal cells, cytokinesis occurs through the formation of a cleavage furrow.
In plant cells, cytokinesis occurs through the formation of a cell plate due to the rigid cell wall.
Errors during mitosis can result in aneuploidy, which is an abnormal number of chromosomes in the daughter cells.
