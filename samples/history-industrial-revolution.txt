The Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain during the mid-18th century around the 1760s.
Britain possessed abundant reserves of coal and iron ore, which provided fuel and materials for new machinery.
James Watt significantly improved the steam engine in 1776, dramatically increasing its efficiency and industrial utility.
The spinning jenny, invented by James Hargreaves in 1764, allowed a single worker to spin multiple spools of thread simultaneously.
Richard Arkwright patented the water frame in 1769, utilizing water power to drive spinning machines in early factories.
The mechanization of textile manufacturing transitioned production from cottage industries into large-scale urban factories.
The development of steam-powered locomotives and railways transformed transportation of raw materials and manufactured goods.
George Stephenson built the Rocket in 1829, which established the viability of steam locomotives on passenger railways.
Rapid urbanization occurred as rural populations migrated to industrial cities like Manchester and Birmingham seeking factory employment.
Early factory workers endured grueling twelve to sixteen hour workdays under hazardous working conditions.
The Factory Act of 1833 in Britain set legal limits on working hours for children in textile factories.
The Industrial Revolution catalyzed the rise of industrial capitalism and a burgeoning middle class alongside a large working class.
